\section{Hemodynamics in abdominal aortic aneurysms}
\label{sec:hemo-aaa}
Current clinical guidelines base the management of AAA mainly on static anatomical parameters, above all the maximum diameter of the aneurysm sac \cite{wanhainen_editors_2024}. The diameter, however, is an imperfect marker of risk: some aneurysms rupture below the intervention threshold, while others remain stable above it \cite{varkevisser_midterm_2020}. This has motivated the search for markers that describe the mechanical state of the aneurysm rather than its size alone \cite{polzer_biomechanical_2020}.

Hemodynamics, the study of blood flow and of the forces it exerts on the vessel walls \cite{secomb_hemodynamics_2016}, is one of the main sources of such markers. Hemodynamic quantities can be measured \textit{in vivo}, for instance with phase-contrast MRI \cite{les_quantification_2010}, or computed with computational fluid dynamics (CFD), which solves the Navier--Stokes equations on patient-specific geometries with physiological boundary conditions (BCs) \cite{katsoudas_hemodynamic_2026}. In AAA research, CFD has received increasing attention because it adds functional information to anatomical imaging: from the same CTA scans used in clinical practice, it quantifies flow patterns, pressures, velocities and wall shear stress. These quantities are being investigated as potential markers of disease progression, with possible prognostic value \cite{bonfanti_personalised_nodate}.
\subsection{Rationale}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Why flow matters: wall shear stress acts on the endothelium and drives wall remodeling and intraluminal thrombus formation. \citeneeded{}
    \item Diameter alone is a limited marker of risk; biomechanical indices are more sensitive in predicting rupture \cite{polzer_biomechanical_2020}.
    \item How and why hemodynamics has been introduced in the study of AAAs. \citeneeded{}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Patient-specific CFD}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Conceptual overview: from the CTA geometry to a patient-specific simulation of blood flow \cite{hu_computational_2025, mutlu_how_2023}.
    \item Main modelling assumptions: rigid wall, Newtonian fluid, outlet models. Details of the pipeline used in this thesis in Chapter~\ref{ch:data}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Wall shear stress indices}
\label{sec:wss-indices}
Let $\boldsymbol{\tau}_w(\mathbf{x},t)$ be the wall shear stress at wall point $\mathbf{x}$, and $[0,T]$ the last cardiac cycle.
All indices are averaged over the cycle \cite{mutlu_integrated_2026, katsoudas_hemodynamic_2026}.
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{TAWSS}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \lVert \boldsymbol{\tau}_w(\mathbf{x},t) \rVert \, dt
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{OSI}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 - \frac{\left\lVert \int_0^T \boldsymbol{\tau}_w(\mathbf{x},t)\,dt \right\rVert}{\int_0^T \lVert \boldsymbol{\tau}_w(\mathbf{x},t) \rVert \, dt}\right)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RRT}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{\left(1 - 2\,\mathrm{OSI}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathrm{TAWSS}(\mathbf{x})},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{ECAP}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathrm{OSI}(\mathbf{x})}{\mathrm{TAWSS}(\mathbf{x})}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item TAWSS: mean intensity of wall shear stress.
    \item OSI: change of direction; 0 = unidirectional, 0.5 = purely oscillatory.
    \item RRT: high where shear stress is low and oscillatory (stagnation).
    \item ECAP: exposure of the wall to low and oscillatory shear, linked to thrombus formation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hemodynamics and AAA progression}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thrombus: links between WSS indices, vortical structures and intraluminal thrombus \cite{zambrano_association_2022, belkacemi_intraluminal_2023}.
    \item Growth: WSS-based and hemodynamic biomarkers predict AAA growth \cite{meyrignac_combining_2020, forneris_ai-powered_2023, kim_deep_2023, rezaeitaleshmahalleh_characterization_2023, joly_cohort_2020, fedotova_association_2025}.
    \item Rupture: blood flow patterns and rupture risk \cite{qiu_association_2022}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hemodynamics after EVAR}
\label{sec:hemo-evar}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Load on the proximal neck and landing zones $\rightarrow$ seal failure (type I endoleak, migration): displacement forces on the endograft depend on curvature and anatomy \cite{figueroa_effect_2009, kandail_patient-specific_2014}; fast estimates of displacement forces have been related to stent-graft-related endoleaks \cite{weng_role_2025}.
    \item In-stent-graft helical flow and risk of migration \cite{tasso_-stent_2019}.
    \item Low and oscillatory flow in the sac $\rightarrow$ thrombus, branch backflow (type II endoleak, sac behaviour). \citeneeded{}
    \item Limitation: each study addresses a single mechanism and a single complication, on small cohorts, and does not combine hemodynamics with other predictors.
\end{itemize}

\section{Predicting adverse events after EVAR}
\label{sec:prediction}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Outcomes used across studies (endoleak, re-intervention, sac growth, mortality) $\rightarrow$ link to the outcome definition in Section~1.2.
    \item Conventional risk scores and their limits \cite{lijftogt_systematic_2017}.
    \item ML models based on clinical, morphological and radiomic features \cite{braak_scoping_2026, karthikesalingam_artificial_2015, long_applying_2024, li_machine_2023}. \citeneeded{Wang 2022}
    \item Reference model reproduced in this thesis: multi-dimensional anatomical features for endoleak prediction \cite{peng_predict_2024} (Appendix~\ref{app:benchmark}).
    \item Recurring limitations: small single-center datasets, no external validation, static descriptors only. None of these models uses hemodynamics.
\end{itemize}

\section{Closest work and research gap}
\label{sec:gap}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Peng et al.\ \cite{peng_endoleak_2026}: point-cloud network (PointNet++ \cite{qi_pointnet_2017}) on morphology, radiomics and CFD; AUC from 0.81 to 0.93.
    \item The +0.12 AUC from CFD is confounded with an architecture change (1 vs.\ 3 feature extractors, completely different feature representation). It cannot show that CFD itself improved the prediction.
    \item One test set, no confidence intervals or paired tests: no uncertainty quantification.
    \item Gap 1: no study isolates the contribution of pre-operative CFD to the prediction of post-EVAR complications with a fixed reference model $\rightarrow$ Q1.
    \item Gap 2: regional means lose \emph{where} on the wall the indices are high or low; a point-based encoder can keep this spatial information \cite{qi_pointnet_2017} $\rightarrow$ Q2.
\end{itemize}
